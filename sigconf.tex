\begin{abstract}
Existing digital journaling approaches place users between two
unsatisfactory extremes. Blank editors preserve freedom but require the writer
to recall, structure, and compose a reflection at once. Fixed templates
reduce this ambiguity but can impose irrelevant questions and a rigid sequence.
AI-assisted tools may produce polished prose, yet opaque generation can
weaken authorship, obscure how input was transformed, and create
privacy uncertainty. We present ReflectBlocks, a human-centered conceptual
design that separates reflection into small, optional, and rearrangeable blocks.
Users begin with one short thought, answer only useful prompts, customize or
remove questions, or switch to unstructured writing. The system organizes the
selected responses only after an explicit request, keeps the resulting entry
fully editable, and links generated passages to their source blocks. This
progressive structure is intended to reduce cognitive load by replacing the
single task of planning and writing a complete entry with smaller decisions,
externalizing the organization of ideas, and allowing users to defer unnecessary
choices. Granular save, export, and deletion controls make the
data lifecycle visible. Our contribution is an inspectable and reversible model
of AI-supported journaling that combines scaffolding with user authorship,
privacy, and control; its cognitive-load benefits remain to be empirically
evaluated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Problem, Users, and Setting}

Personal reflection often begins with a small, time-sensitive thought: a person
notices that a presentation made them nervous, that a conversation was
uncomfortable, or that an ordinary event taught them something. Capturing that
first thought may require only one sentence. Developing it into a useful journal
entry, however, requires the writer to recall what happened, name a feeling,
consider its cause, identify a lesson, and decide what to do next. The design
problem addressed in this project is the gap between these two activities. A
writer may want to reflect without having the time, energy, or immediate
structure needed to turn a brief thought into a coherent account.

ReflectBlocks is a proposed personal reflection tool for people who want to
record and develop such thoughts while retaining control of their writing. Its
primary users are not defined by age or occupation; they are defined by the
activity under study: they voluntarily journal or want to reflect on meaningful
events, but sometimes experience friction when beginning or structuring an
entry. The focal unit of analysis is one individual reflection episode, from
the moment an event prompts a thought to the point at which the writer chooses
to save, export, or discard the material. The anticipated setting is private
and self-directed use on a personal phone or computer, often shortly after an
event but potentially later when the user has more time.

The interaction begins with a short phrase such as ``I felt nervous during
today's presentation.'' ReflectBlocks can then offer optional prompts---for
example, what happened, how the user felt, why the event mattered, what they
learned, and what they might do differently. These prompts are represented as
independent blocks. The writer may answer, edit, remove, add, skip, or rearrange
them, or bypass them entirely and use a blank editor. Only after an explicit
request does the system organize the selected material into a draft journal
entry. That draft remains editable, and source indicators can show which blocks
contributed to each generated passage. Saving the blocks, saving or exporting
the entry, deleting it permanently, and saving nothing are all intended to be
visible user choices.

This project treats those proposed capabilities as design hypotheses rather
than established user requirements. In particular, the project description
does not yet demonstrate that writers want generated prose, that the suggested
questions match their reflection practices, or that source indicators provide
meaningful transparency. A human-centered process must investigate those
questions through evidence about people and practice before the concept is
treated as a solution \cite{preece2023interaction}. The design therefore
separates the underlying opportunity---supporting a transition from a fleeting
thought to deliberate reflection---from the particular block-based interface
currently proposed to address it.

\subsection{Research Questions and Contribution}

The study is guided by three questions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1:} How do people currently capture a brief reaction to an
  event and develop it into a more considered personal reflection?
  \item \textbf{RQ2:} What forms of optional structure, authorship control, and
  privacy protection should shape a tool that supports this transition?
  \item \textbf{RQ3:} How can a conceptual model make the relationship between
  a writer's original material and system-organized prose understandable,
  editable, and reversible?
\end{itemize}

The intended contribution is an evidence-traceable set of requirements and a
conceptual design for ReflectBlocks. The concept combines progressive
reflection prompts, a blank-writing alternative, explicit rather than automatic
generation, editable output, provenance between source blocks and generated
text, and granular retention controls. The contribution is not a claim that a
block interface improves wellbeing or writing quality. At this stage, it is a
bounded account of a design opportunity and a set of assumptions that the
subsequent study must confirm, refine, or reject.

\section{Discovering Requirements}
\label{sec:discovering}

\subsection{Stakeholders, Assumptions, and Problem Space}

Requirements discovery identifies the people, activities, values, and
constraints that define a design space \cite{preece2023interaction}. For
ReflectBlocks, the primary stakeholder is the writer whose experiences and
language become the content of an entry. This person has the greatest stake in
whether the system preserves meaning, protects sensitive material, and allows
them to change their mind. A trusted friend, teacher, mentor, or other recipient
becomes a secondary stakeholder only when the writer chooses to export and
share an entry; private writing does not imply that another person is entitled
to access it.

Technical and organizational actors also influence the experience even when
they never read an entry directly. ReflectBlocks developers determine the
available controls and defaults. A storage provider may hold saved blocks or
entries, and an AI provider may process text when generation is requested.
These actors control infrastructure and policy, while the writer bears much of
the consequence of unauthorized disclosure, unclear retention, or distorted
output. Privacy and security specialists can examine the data lifecycle, and
people with expertise in journaling, writing, or wellbeing can identify harmful
assumptions in prompts. Such experts are advisers rather than substitutes for
the people who will actually use the system.

The initial concept rests on five assumptions. First, some prospective users
can capture a short thought more readily than they can begin a complete journal
entry. Second, prompts can support reflection when they are relevant and
optional. Third, users want assistance with organization without surrendering
authorship of the final text. Fourth, showing the source of generated passages
can help users inspect and correct the output. Fifth, clear storage, processing,
export, and deletion controls are necessary when entries contain personal
information. None of these statements is yet a finding. Each is recorded as a
provisional claim to be examined during data gathering rather than embedded
silently as a design fact.

These interests do not always align. A provider may prefer to retain data for
reliability, analytics, or model improvement, while a writer may expect an
unsaved or deleted thought to disappear. A system may favor smooth automation,
while the writer may need an interruption that makes AI processing and its
consequences visible. A generated entry may sound polished but introduce an
interpretation the writer did not intend. ReflectBlocks therefore treats
control as more than the presence of an edit button. The writer must be able to
understand what will happen, initiate consequential actions, inspect the result,
revise or reject it, and remove stored material. These principles follow the
broader HCI goal of making the conceptual model and available actions
understandable to the person using the system \cite{norman2013design}.

The problem space is deliberately narrower than digital mental-health support.
ReflectBlocks does not diagnose emotions, recommend treatment, evaluate whether
a reaction is correct, or intervene in a crisis. It should not infer that every
entry represents a stable psychological state. Prompts may help a writer examine
an experience, but they cannot guarantee insight or benefit. This boundary is
important because positioning a reflective writing tool as therapy would create
different safety, expertise, and evaluation requirements than those supported
by the present project.

\subsection{Current Practice and Opportunity Areas}

Because fieldwork has not yet been reported, the following journey is a
provisional current-practice model, not an observed universal sequence. A
prospective user experiences an event and decides that it is worth remembering.
They may write a sentence in a notes application, open a paper or digital
journal, use a fixed template, or postpone writing. If they continue, they must
decide which details matter, find words for reactions that may still be unclear,
and organize those fragments into an entry. Finally, they choose whether to
keep the writing private, share it, or discard it.

This journey exposes several possible breakdowns for investigation. The
motivation to capture the event may be brief, while composing a full entry
takes sustained attention. A blank page provides freedom but little support for
deciding what to explore. A fixed template provides structure but may ask
irrelevant questions or impose an order that does not match the writer's
experience. Postponing the task may reduce immediate effort but can also leave
only a minimal note whose meaning is difficult for the writer to reconstruct
later. These are reasoned possibilities derived from the proposed concept, not
participant findings; interviews and observation must establish when, for whom,
and with what consequences they actually occur. Contextual inquiry is relevant
because it can examine decisions near the situated activity rather than asking
people only to speculate about a finished interface \cite{beyer1998contextual}.

Three preliminary opportunity areas follow from this model. The first is
\emph{low-friction capture with progressive support}. A one-sentence starting
point could preserve the immediacy of a thought, while optional blocks could
help the writer expand it at their own pace. The key need is not necessarily a
particular set of cards; it is the ability to move between minimal capture,
guided reflection, and unstructured writing without losing work or being forced
through every step.

The second opportunity is \emph{assistance that preserves authorship}. Explicit
generation, editable output, and links from generated prose back to source
blocks could make organization inspectable rather than magical. The design
must still determine whether writers understand these links, whether generated
language changes the meaning or tone of their account, and whether a polished
draft discourages substantive editing. A successful interaction would let the
writer treat generation as a reversible drafting operation, not as an
authoritative interpretation of their experience.

The third opportunity is \emph{privacy as an interaction rather than a hidden
policy}. Before submitting personal text for processing, a user should be able
to learn what data will leave the device, why it is needed, where it will be
stored, and how it can be deleted. Retention choices should distinguish original
blocks from the generated entry and allow the user to save neither. These
controls create design questions about comprehensible wording, safe defaults,
confirmation of permanent deletion, and the behavior of backups or external AI
services.

Together, the three areas define the problem space carried into data gathering:
supporting the transition from a fleeting thought to deeper reflection,
maintaining the writer's authorship throughout system assistance, and making
the lifecycle of sensitive text understandable and controllable. The next stage
must test these opportunities against actual practices, including people who
prefer blank writing, people who reject generated prose, and people for whom
privacy concerns outweigh the value of automated organization.

\section{Data Gathering}

\subsection{Method Rationale and Study Design}

We chose interviews because they let people describe, in their own words, what
actually happens when they try to journal. We added a short card exercise at
the end of each interview: participants looked at sample ReflectBlocks
prompts on paper cards and said which ones felt useful and which did not.
This gave us a reaction to something concrete, not just a memory
\cite{preece2023interaction}.

We recruited eight participants from our families and extended family
networks in Bangladesh, especially parents and elderly relatives, all of them above age 45.
Recruitment and fieldwork took place over a two-month period. We looked for
people who journal in some form (paper, an app, voice notes) at least
sometimes, or who had tried and stopped. We wanted a mix of habits, not a large
or representative sample. Eight people took part: five
journal on and off, two used to journal but stopped in the last year, and one
writes daily using a fixed template. A ninth person had to cancel and was not
replaced. Because everyone who joined was already willing to talk about
journaling, our findings say less about people who have never tried it at all.

Before the interviews, we wrote down three things we were assuming: that a
short thought is easier to start than a full entry, that prompts only help
when they feel relevant, and that people want help organizing their writing
without losing control over it. After the interviews we checked each
assumption against what participants actually said.

\subsection{Procedure, Ethics, and Data Management}

Each session took about 20 minutes and was done in person, usually by visiting
participants at their homes. All interviews were conducted in Bangla, and the
researchers' written notes were later translated into English. Participant statements reported here are paraphrased from the
translated notes rather than presented as verbatim quotations. After consent, participants
answered a few quick questions about their journaling habits, did the main
interview (guide in Appendix
\ref{app:materials}), and then the card exercise. We took written session notes and kept the marked-up cards. Taking
part was voluntary: participants could skip a question or stop at any time,
and no one received payment or course credit for taking part. One person
skipped the card exercise because they were short on time but still did the
full interview.

We opened with simple, open questions like ``Tell me about the last time you
wrote about something that happened to you,'' then followed up with ``What
happened next?'' Asking about a real, specific moment worked better than
asking about habits in general. We tested the interview once with a lab
colleague first: the original opening question (``Tell me about your
reflection habits'') got vague answers, so we changed it to ask about one
specific time instead.

\section{Data Analysis, Interpretation, and Presentation}

\subsection{Preparation and Analytic Approach}

We used thematic analysis on the eight sets of interview notes and the marked-up
cards. First we read through each participant's interview notes and wrote a short summary of it.
Then we labeled interesting parts with short codes, compared codes across
participants, and grouped similar codes into themes. We checked each theme
against the original interview notes to make sure it still matched what people
actually said \cite{braun2006thematic}.

Two researchers independently coded the notes from the first three interviews and then compared
their coding. Some codes did not match at first, mostly around whether something
counted as a ``workaround'' or just plain ``friction,'' so we talked it
through and agreed on one shared set of codes. One person then coded the rest
and checked back on anything unclear. When a participant said something that
did not fit a theme, we kept it as an exception instead of ignoring it, and
those exceptions are noted in the findings below.

\subsection{Findings}
\label{sec:findings}

\paragraph{Theme 1: People lose the thought before they even start writing.}
Most participants said the hard part was not writing, it was getting to a
journal in time. P03 described having thoughts while travelling or showering
that were gone, or felt like homework, by the time they sat down at a laptop.
Six of eight participants described the same thing: they had a thought, but
by the time they sat down to write, it had faded. This tells us the design
should focus on capturing a thought fast, before worrying about structure.

\paragraph{Theme 2: Prompts help, but only sometimes.} Most participants liked
having optional prompts, but only when the prompts matched how they felt. P02
described the ``what did you learn'' prompt as nice sometimes, but at other
times felt it pushed them to produce a lesson when they only wanted to vent.
One participant, P05, was the opposite: they used a fixed template every day
and liked always seeing the same questions. This tells us prompts should be
easy to skip, and that one default will not work for everyone.

\paragraph{Theme 3: People want to know what the system changed.} Participants
were fine with the system organizing their writing, as long as they could
tell what was still their own words. P06 described wanting to know exactly
which words were their own whenever the system changed even a little of
their text. Seven of eight
participants wanted a way to compare the generated text with what they
originally wrote, and one participant (P08) said they would only ever use
blank writing, no generated text at all. This tells us showing the source of
generated text is not a nice-to-have, and blank writing needs to stay a real
option, not just a backup.

\section{Establishing Requirements}
\label{sec:requirements}

\subsection{From Findings to Requirements}

Requirements state what a system and its surrounding service must enable or
respect; they should not prematurely prescribe a screen layout
\cite{preece2023interaction}. We translated each of the three themes from
Section~\ref{sec:findings}, together with the stakeholder concerns and
assumptions from Section~\ref{sec:discovering}, into requirements by naming
the actor, the condition under which it applies, the action or quality
required, and a success criterion. Because our sample was eight family and
relative participants, all above age 45, we treated a requirement as
supported only when at least two participants independently pointed to the
same need, or when a single participant's account was corroborated by the
card exercise or by a stakeholder concern already identified in
Section~\ref{sec:discovering}. Requirements were then checked informally
against the original interview notes and against the stakeholder table
(Table~\ref{tab:stakeholders}) rather than through a separate participant
workshop, which this study did not have time or budget to run; this is a
limitation we return to in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

Functional requirements describe supported actions. Data requirements
identify the information needed, its source, ownership, sensitivity, and
lifecycle. Quality (non-functional) requirements express properties such as
accessibility, privacy, reliability, and learnability. Organizational
requirements describe policy, review, or governance conditions. The
categories interact in this project: source-transparency (Theme~3) is at
once a functional requirement (show provenance), a data requirement (which
text is sent where), and a trust/privacy quality that the writer must be
able to verify.

\subsection{Prioritization and Acceptance Criteria}

We prioritized requirements using MoSCoW, applied by the two researchers
together rather than by a separate stakeholder workshop. A requirement was
marked \textit{Must} when at least half of participants described the same
breakdown or when omitting it would let the system act on a writer's private
text without their knowledge (e.g., sending data or generating text without
consent). \textit{Should} items address a real but less universal need or a
quality concern raised by only one or two participants. \textit{Could} items
depend on later access to expert review that this study did not yet obtain.

For each \textit{Must} requirement we specify a testable acceptance
criterion:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{R1.} Given a new one-sentence thought, a writer can save it
  to the app in under 5 seconds, without first selecting a prompt, block, or
  template.
  \item \textbf{R4.} Given a set of filled or partially filled blocks, no
  organized draft is produced until the writer makes a separate, explicit
  generation request, even after closing and reopening the app.
  \item \textbf{R7.} Given a generation request, the AI provider receives
  only the blocks the writer selected for that request, never the writer's
  other saved blocks or past entries.
  \item \textbf{R8.} Given a saved block or a generated entry, a writer can
  delete it independently of other saved material and receive confirmation
  that the deletion is permanent.
  \item \textbf{R12.} Given a freshly generated entry, a writer can change,
  delete, or add any sentence, and the edited version, not the original
  generated output, is what gets saved or exported.
\end{itemize}

Three requirements remain provisional. \textbf{R3} is flagged for validation
because it rests on a single counterexample (P05) against the majority
pattern in Theme~2, so we do not yet know how common a fixed-prompt
preference is. \textbf{R9} is flagged because our entire sample was recruited
from family and relatives above age 45; the accessibility need it describes
may not generalize to younger or more technically experienced writers, and
could equally understate needs we did not observe in this sample.
\textbf{R13} is the weakest-evidenced requirement in the catalogue: block
customization (editing a block's question, adding a custom block, reordering
blocks) appears in the original ReflectBlocks concept in Section~1, but no
participant raised it unprompted, and our interview guide and card exercise
did not ask about it directly. We kept it as a low-priority, explicitly
unvalidated entry rather than dropping it, so the gap is visible instead of
silently resolved. All three are carried forward as open questions for the
evaluation plan in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} rather than treated as
settled.

\section{Conceptual Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Design Vision and Conceptual Model}

The conceptual design proposes ReflectBlocks, a private reflective-writing app
for a personal phone or computer that helps a writer move from a single
fleeting thought to a reviewed, editable journal entry without forcing a
choice between a blank page and a fixed template. Its central design
principle, derived from R1, R4, R12, and Theme~3 (\S\ref{sec:findings}), is
that structure and AI assistance stay optional, requested, and reversible: the
writer decides when a block is used and when text is generated, and every
generated sentence remains attributable to its source and editable rather than
authoritative.

The system model is an object/action model built on four named objects: a
\textit{thought} (the one-line capture that opens an episode), a
\textit{block} (an independent, optional prompt-and-answer pair such as ``What
happened?''), a \textit{draft entry} (organized text produced only on
request), and a \textit{source link} (the visible connection between a
passage in the draft and the block it came from). A writer can capture a
thought, answer, skip, edit a block's question, dismiss, add a custom block,
or reorder a block (R1, R2, R13), switch to
unstructured writing at any point (R6), request generation once blocks are
ready (R4), inspect which block produced which sentence (R5), edit or remove
any part of the result (R12), and independently choose what to save, export,
or discard (R8, R14). Naming these four objects consistently lets a writer
predict, before acting, what will happen: touching a block never sends data
anywhere, and only the explicit generate action does (R7, R10)
\cite{norman2013design}.

The proposed interaction is a hybrid of form fill-in and direct manipulation,
not a conversational agent. Blocks behave like a small set of independent
cards a writer can tap, type into, remove, or drag into a new order; the draft
entry behaves like an ordinary editable document, not a chat transcript. This
fits participants who wanted tap-and-type actions rather than a new gesture or
menu (R9). A conversational
interface was considered and rejected for the primary flow (\S\ref{sec:altdesign}) because it
would hide the block objects the design otherwise keeps visible. ReflectBlocks
deliberately does not interpret why a writer feels a certain way, infer a
psychological state, or decide what an entry means; it only reorganizes the
writer's own words on request, consistent with the boundary already set in
Table~\ref{tab:scope}.

\subsection{Scenario and Task Flow}

\paragraph{Scenario: P03's evening entry.} P03 has the kind of moment she
described in her interview: a thought arrives sharp and specific while she is
in the shower or on the bus, but by the time she reaches her laptop that
evening it has already begun to fade (T1). She opens ReflectBlocks and
captures one line before anything else loads, ``felt uneasy after this
morning's conversation, don't want to lose that,'' in under five seconds
without first choosing a prompt (R1). Only once that line is saved does the
system surface three suggested blocks---``What happened?'', ``How did you
feel?'', and ``Why did it affect you?''---and leave two lower-priority ones,
``What did you learn?'' and ``What would you do differently?'', collapsed but
visible, so that structure never competes with the moment of capture itself.
She answers the first three and dismisses the last two with one tap each,
because the event still feels unresolved (R2). When ready, she taps
``Organize my entry,'' an action separate from filling in blocks, so nothing
is generated automatically while she is still typing (R4). ReflectBlocks
shows a short, plain-language notice that only her three answered blocks,
not her past entries, will be sent for organizing, and asks her to confirm
(R7, R10). The system returns a draft entry with each sentence visibly tagged
to the block it came from (R5); she notices one generated sentence
oversimplifies her feeling and edits it directly, so the edited sentence,
not the original generated one, becomes part of the entry (R12). She ends
the session by choosing to save only her original blocks, not the generated
entry, because she is not yet ready to commit to the organized wording (R14);
the choice is confirmed before anything is written to storage. Because
ReflectBlocks is intended for private, self-directed use on a personal device
that other household members may occasionally borrow or glance at
(Table~\ref{tab:scope}; \S\ref{sec:discovering}), no entry content, generated
or otherwise, appears in a notification or lock-screen preview regardless of
whether P03 is currently signed in, and closing the app returns to a neutral
home screen rather than the last open entry.

\paragraph{Exception: an empty generation request.} If P03 dismisses every
suggested block and taps ``Organize my entry'' with nothing answered beyond
her original one-line thought, ReflectBlocks does not attempt to expand that
line into a fabricated account. Generating prose from a single sentence would
have no block to attribute it to, which would violate the source-link
guarantee in R5 before the entry even exists. Instead the system explains, in
the same plain language used elsewhere, that there is nothing yet to
organize, and returns her to the block view rather than silently producing
text she never wrote.

\paragraph{Exception: an interrupted session.} If P03 is interrupted while
answering a block---someone else needs the phone, or she is called
away---anything already typed is kept locally without requiring a separate
save action. Because no block content is transmitted anywhere until she
explicitly requests generation (R7), an interruption before that point
carries no disclosure risk beyond the device itself; she can resume, discard,
or switch to blank writing when she returns, and nothing has left the app in
the meantime.

\subsection{Design Alternatives and Trade-Offs}
\label{sec:altdesign}

We considered a conversational assistant that would ask follow-up questions in
a chat thread and progressively compose the entry turn by turn. This was
rejected for the primary flow: it would make generation continuous rather than
explicit, working against R4 and against Theme~3's finding that participants
wanted to know precisely what the system changed; P08's flat refusal to use
any generated text, preferring blank writing only, suggested that the dialogue
framing itself, not only output quality, was a source of distrust. The block-and-request model was
retained because it keeps composition visible as a sequence of discrete,
inspectable writer actions rather than a single opaque exchange.

We also considered generating the draft entry automatically as soon as every
suggested block was filled, removing the separate ``organize'' action to save
a tap. This was rejected because it would remove the moment at which the
writer currently consents to sending data externally (R7, R10) and would
contradict R6, under which blank-writing participants such as P08 must be able
to complete and save an entry without generation ever being invoked. An
explicit request adds one interaction step but preserves the consent boundary
that Theme~3 and the stakeholder analysis (\S\ref{sec:discovering}) identified
as a condition for trust, at some cost to writers such as P05 who wanted a
faster, always-the-same-questions default (R3); we accommodate that case with
a persistent personal default rather than automatic generation, so speed does
not require giving up the consent step.

Finally, we considered removing the blank-writing mode to simplify the
interface around blocks, since blocks were the original project concept. This
was rejected because Theme~3 and R6 both trace directly to a participant (P08)
who would use no other mode, and because demoting blank writing to a hidden
fallback would reproduce the exact asymmetry participants criticized.
Participatory input from participants who rejected generated prose entirely
was therefore treated as a design constraint, not a minority preference to be
designed around \cite{muller1993participatory}. The remaining trade-off is
organizational: supporting two entry modes and a provenance layer increases
what the development team must build and test, against a single-mode design
that would be simpler to ship but would not accommodate the writers this study
evidenced.

Beyond these three choices, several trade-offs cut across all of them and
remain open questions for the evaluation plan rather than settled design
decisions. The retained model asks more of a writer at each decision point
(which blocks to keep, whether to generate, which of four save outcomes to
choose) than a single fixed form would; this trades convenience for the
control Theme~3 identified as necessary for trust, but has not been tested on
a writer under time pressure or low motivation, where the added decisions
could themselves reproduce the delayed-capture problem in Theme~1. R9 is the
design's most direct attempt to keep that cost low across the full aged-45+
sample it is evidenced from, including a participant such as P08, who was
both the most skeptical of generation and the most likely to be discouraged
by a busy interface; whether the guided/blank-mode choice is genuinely as
reachable for a participant like him as it is for a more habitual app user
remains untested rather than assumed here. R5's requirement that a writer be
able to view which words came from their own blocks versus system-organized
language is also more demanding for this study's bilingual participants than
it would be for an English-only sample: since every interview was conducted
in Bangla, any language smoothing the system performs when organizing Bangla
or mixed-language answers falls under ``system-organized language'' and would
need the same visible, distinguishable treatment as any other generated
wording. Whether the current source-link mechanism achieves that specifically
for language changes, as opposed to pure reorganization, was not tested
separately and remains a question for the evaluation plan rather than a
settled claim. Finally, the retained design has more states
to fail in than a fixed form does, which is why the scenario above specifies
explicit recovery behavior for an empty generation request and an interrupted
writing session rather than leaving those cases undefined.

\section{Evaluation Plan}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Evaluation Questions and Methods}

The next iteration will evaluate whether people can understand and use the
ReflectBlocks conceptual model well enough to satisfy its priority requirements.
The aim will not be to ask whether participants simply like the interface.
Instead, the evaluation will focus on whether writers can complete the main
reflection flow, predict what the system will do, recover from unwanted choices,
and understand when their text is processed or retained. This follows directly
from the requirement-to-design mapping in Table~\ref{tab:designmapping}.

Three questions will guide the evaluation. First, can writers capture a thought
quickly and choose how much structure they want without being forced through
prompts or AI generation? Second, can writers understand when generation occurs,
which information is used, where generated wording came from, and how to change
that wording? Third, can writers understand the save and deletion choices,
including what information remains after each choice? The evaluation will also
revisit R3, R9, and R13 because Section~\ref{sec:requirements} identified these
requirements as provisional rather than settled.

We will use a clickable low-fidelity prototype in a moderated think-aloud
walkthrough. Think-aloud methods are widely used in usability evaluation because
participants verbalize what they are noticing and thinking while performing a
task; however, the evaluator's prompts can influence what participants say
\cite{mcdonald2012thinkaloud}. The moderator will therefore give the task first
and use neutral reminders such as ``Please keep talking'' instead of directing
participants toward a particular control. A low-fidelity prototype is appropriate
at this stage because the main questions concern interaction sequence, labels,
feedback, and the visibility of important choices rather than implementation
details \cite{rettig1994prototyping}.

We plan to recruit eight people who currently journal, have journaled
occasionally, or have previously tried and stopped. The earlier study consisted
of family and relative participants above age 45, so the evaluation will
deliberately include variation in age and experience with text-editing
applications. This will help us examine R9 rather than assuming that the
interaction needs observed in the original sample apply equally to other
writers. Participants will not be required to describe a real sensitive event.
Tasks will use a neutral fictional situation, such as feeling nervous after a
presentation, and participants may invent all journal content.

The walkthrough will use five user tasks. First, the participant will start from
the ReflectBlocks entry screen and will be asked to record one short thought.
We will measure the interaction time from the appearance of the entry screen to
the saved thought, excluding device or application loading time, and record
hesitation, errors, and requests for help (R1, R9).

Second, the prototype will show several reflection blocks, including one prompt
that is intentionally inappropriate for the scenario. The participant will be
asked to continue reflecting in whatever way they prefer. We will observe
whether they can dismiss the irrelevant block, ignore the remaining prompts,
move to blank writing, or use the available preference and customization
controls without assuming that any of these actions is required (R2, R3, R6,
R13).

Third, after several blocks contain sample responses, the participant will be
asked to organize the material into a draft. Before they continue, the prototype
will display the planned processing notice. We will ask the participant to
explain, in their own words, whether generation has already occurred, which
blocks will be processed, who will receive them, and why they are being sent
(R4, R7, R10). Privacy-notice research shows that simply displaying a notice
does not guarantee useful understanding, so the evaluation will measure what the
participant actually understood from it \cite{schaub2015privacy}.

Fourth, the prototype will show a prepared generated entry containing source
links and one sentence that deliberately changes the tone of the original
material. The participant will be asked to identify where that sentence came
from and then correct, remove, or replace it. This task tests whether source
transparency is understandable and whether generated language remains practically
reversible (R5, R12). These questions are also consistent with established
human--AI interaction guidance that emphasizes making AI behavior understandable
and giving users ways to correct unwanted system output
\cite{amershi2019guidelines}.

Finally, the participant will be given several retention requests, such as
`keep the original blocks but not the generated entry'' and `delete this block
but keep the completed entry.'' After each action, the participant will be asked
what they believe still exists. This task will test the mental model of
independent saving, export, and deletion (R8, R14), including the confirmation
shown before an irreversible deletion.

The clickable prototype will intentionally use prepared text rather than sending
participants' writing to a real AI service. It can therefore test the sequence

users' understanding of retention choices. It cannot establish the quality or
latency of a real language model, whether a production backend actually sends
only selected blocks, whether deletion is permanent across servers and backups,
or whether stored data are secure. R7 and R8 will therefore require technical
verification in addition to the user-facing walkthrough before they can be
claimed as implemented.

\subsection{Success, Risk, and Iteration}

Evaluation success will be based on the requirement-level decision rules in
Table~\ref{tab:evaluation}, not on general satisfaction. For the main interaction
tasks, we will look for successful completion without the moderator telling the
participant which control to use. We will also record misunderstandings,
workarounds, hesitation, and unexpected ways of using the design and link each
observation back to the relevant requirement.

Privacy and irreversible data loss will be treated more strictly than ordinary
navigation problems. A participant misunderstanding whether text is about to be
sent externally, or misunderstanding what an irreversible deletion will remove,
will be treated as a critical breakdown even if the participant eventually
finishes the task. Such a breakdown will require revision of the relevant notice,
label, sequence, or confirmation before that part of the design is carried
forward.

R3 and R13 will be evaluated as open design questions rather than features that
must be defended. If participants do not find a clear use for persistent prompt
preferences or block customization, these controls may be simplified, deferred,
or removed. Similarly, barriers that occur mainly among participants with less
text-editing experience will trigger revision of R9 and the initial interaction
rather than being dismissed as participant error.

After each revision, the affected task will be tested again before the design is
treated as stable. The purpose of this evaluation is therefore to reduce the
remaining uncertainty around ReflectBlocks and decide what should change next,
not to claim that one low-fidelity study proves that the final system is usable
for every writer.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}

\subsection{What the Process Established}

This project established that the gap between a fleeting thought and a
composed journal entry is not primarily a writing-skill problem but a
sequencing problem: writers lose the thought while structure, tone, and
completeness are still being decided (Theme~1), and the requirements that
followed (R1, R9) shape ReflectBlocks around capturing that thought before
anything else is asked of the writer. Two further findings pulled in the
same direction without collapsing into one requirement each. Theme~2 showed
that optional structure is only sometimes wanted, so the design responds not
with one prompt set but with a dismissable, per-writer-default block model
(R2, R3). Theme~3 showed that writers who accept AI assistance still
insist on knowing what it changed, and one writer (P08) will not accept
generated prose under any framing; the conceptual design responds with
explicit, on-request generation, visible source links, fully editable
output, and a blank-writing mode that is a first-class alternative rather
than a fallback (R4, R5, R6, R12). The data-lifecycle and privacy
requirements (R7, R8, R10, R14) were not traced to a theme in the same
direct sense, but to the stakeholder analysis and initial assumptions in
Section~\ref{sec:discovering}; the conceptual design treats them as equally
binding, since a writer who cannot predict what happens to their text after
generation has no real authorship over it, regardless of how the editor
behaves.

The value of this work lies in the traceable chain from eight family and
relative interviews, through three themes and a fourteen-item requirements
catalogue, to a conceptual model and scenario that a future evaluation can
directly test (Table~\ref{tab:requirements}, Table~\ref{tab:designmapping}).
It is not a claim that this design will reduce the burden of reflective
writing, that a block-based interface is preferable to a blank page for
most writers, or that findings from eight Bangla-speaking relatives above
age 45 describe reflective writers generally. Table~\ref{tab:claims}
separates what the study supports from what remains open.

\subsection{Limitations and Next Steps}
\label{sec:limitations}

The study is limited first by sample and recruitment: all eight
participants were family members or relatives of the research team, all
above age 45, recruited in Bangladesh over a two-month window. This
closeness may have made participants more willing to discuss private
writing habits than strangers would be, but it may equally have made them
less willing to voice sharp criticism of a concept proposed by a relative;
we cannot separate these effects from the interview notes alone. The
sample also cannot speak to younger writers, writers outside Bangladesh, or
writers who have never attempted journaling, all of whom are outside the
population these findings describe.

The study is limited second by method. Interviews and a card-reaction
exercise reveal what participants say about their practice, not what they
would actually do with a working prototype; the near-instant capture claim
behind R1, for instance, was never timed. As noted in
Section~\ref{sec:requirements}, requirements were checked against
transcripts and the stakeholder table rather than through a separate
stakeholder or expert workshop, so R11's review step and the organizational
requirements more broadly rest on the research team's own judgment rather
than a domain expert's. Findings should therefore be read as a bounded,
evidence-traceable account of eight people's stated practice and reaction
to a proposed concept, not as a validated design.

The next iteration should prioritize the three requirements already flagged
as provisional in Section~\ref{sec:requirements}: whether a persistent
prompt default (R3) is common beyond one counterexample, whether the
accessibility requirement (R9) generalizes past an aged-45+ sample, and
whether block customization (R13) is wanted at all, since no participant
raised it unprompted. The evaluation plan in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}
is designed to produce that evidence through walkthrough tasks rather than
further interviews alone. The team's own position as relatives of the
participants, and as the sole coders of the interview data, is itself a
limitation of the analysis that produced Table~\ref{tab:evidence} and the
three themes in Section~\ref{sec:findings}; the coding disagreement noted
during analysis was resolved by the same two researchers who conducted the
interviews, so an independent coder or reviewer is a concrete next step,
not only a caveat.

\subsection{Conclusion}

Writers in this study did not lack the ability to write; they lacked a way
to begin before a fleeting reaction faded, and a way to accept help
organizing that reaction without losing track of which words were theirs.
ReflectBlocks responds to that specific gap with a fourteen-item,
evidence-traced requirements catalogue and a conceptual design that keeps
capture, structure, generation, and retention as separate, writer-initiated
steps rather than one automated pipeline. The contribution is the traceable
path from eight interviews to that design, not a demonstration that the
design works: the requirements table marks which claims are well supported
and which rest on a single participant or on the team's own assumptions,
and the evaluation plan in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} is built to test
the Must requirements before any of the Should or Could items receive
further design attention. A future designer picking up this project should
treat the provisional requirements in Table~\ref{tab:claims} as the first
thing to test, not the model in Figure~\ref{fig:designmodel} as a finished
answer.

\subsection{Participant Information and Consent Checklist}

Before beginning, provide: the study purpose; what participation involves;
duration; note-taking and data handling; foreseeable discomforts; benefits, if
any; voluntary participation and withdrawal process; contact details; and the
applicable approval statement. Do not include a participant's name, signature,
contact information, or other identifying data in this submitted report.

\subsection{Semi-Structured Interview Guide}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Tell me about the last time you [performed the focal activity]. What
  happened from beginning to end?
  \item What parts were easy, difficult, slow, risky, or frustrating? Can you
  show me an example?
  \item Which people, tools, documents, and information did you use? What
  happened when one was unavailable?
  \item What workarounds have you developed, and what do they cost you or
  others?
  \item If a new system supported this activity, what would it need to respect
  about your practice, privacy, access, or control?
  \item Is there anything important we did not ask? Which of your answers should
  we treat cautiously?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Interviewer reminders.} Ask neutral follow-ups (``Can you say
more?''); do not promise a feature; do not pressure participants to disclose
sensitive information; record analytic memos separately from factual notes.
