\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Problem, Users, and Setting}

Personal reflection often begins with a small, time-sensitive thought: a person
notices that a presentation made them nervous, that a conversation was
uncomfortable, or that an ordinary event taught them something. Capturing that
first thought may require only one sentence. Developing it into a useful journal
entry, however, requires the writer to recall what happened, name a feeling,
consider its cause, identify a lesson, and decide what to do next. The design
problem addressed in this project is the gap between these two activities. A
writer may want to reflect without having the time, energy, or immediate
structure needed to turn a brief thought into a coherent account.

ReflectBlocks is a proposed personal reflection tool for people who want to
record and develop such thoughts while retaining control of their writing. Its
primary users are not defined by age or occupation; they are defined by the
activity under study: they voluntarily journal or want to reflect on meaningful
events, but sometimes experience friction when beginning or structuring an
entry. The focal unit of analysis is one individual reflection episode, from
the moment an event prompts a thought to the point at which the writer chooses
to save, export, or discard the material. The anticipated setting is private
and self-directed use on a personal phone or computer, often shortly after an
event but potentially later when the user has more time.

The interaction begins with a short phrase such as ``I felt nervous during
today's presentation.'' ReflectBlocks can then offer optional prompts---for
example, what happened, how the user felt, why the event mattered, what they
learned, and what they might do differently. These prompts are represented as
independent blocks. The writer may answer, edit, remove, add, skip, or rearrange
them, or bypass them entirely and use a blank editor. Only after an explicit
request does the system organize the selected material into a draft journal
entry. That draft remains editable, and source indicators can show which blocks
contributed to each generated passage. Saving the blocks, saving or exporting
the entry, deleting it permanently, and saving nothing are all intended to be
visible user choices.

This project treats those proposed capabilities as design hypotheses rather
than established user requirements. In particular, the project description
does not yet demonstrate that writers want generated prose, that the suggested
questions match their reflection practices, or that source indicators provide
meaningful transparency. A human-centered process must investigate those
questions through evidence about people and practice before the concept is
treated as a solution \cite{preece2023interaction}. The design therefore
separates the underlying opportunity---supporting a transition from a fleeting
thought to deliberate reflection---from the particular block-based interface
currently proposed to address it.

\subsection{Research Questions and Contribution}

The study is guided by three questions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1:} How do people currently capture a brief reaction to an
  event and develop it into a more considered personal reflection?
  \item \textbf{RQ2:} What forms of optional structure, authorship control, and
  privacy protection should shape a tool that supports this transition?
  \item \textbf{RQ3:} How can a conceptual model make the relationship between
  a writer's original material and system-organized prose understandable,
  editable, and reversible?
\end{itemize}

The intended contribution is an evidence-traceable set of requirements and a
conceptual design for ReflectBlocks. The concept combines progressive
reflection prompts, a blank-writing alternative, explicit rather than automatic
generation, editable output, provenance between source blocks and generated
text, and granular retention controls. The contribution is not a claim that a
block interface improves wellbeing or writing quality. At this stage, it is a
bounded account of a design opportunity and a set of assumptions that the
subsequent study must confirm, refine, or reject.

\section{Discovering Requirements}
\label{sec:discovering}

\subsection{Stakeholders, Assumptions, and Problem Space}

Requirements discovery identifies the people, activities, values, and
constraints that define a design space \cite{preece2023interaction}. For
ReflectBlocks, the primary stakeholder is the writer whose experiences and
language become the content of an entry. This person has the greatest stake in
whether the system preserves meaning, protects sensitive material, and allows
them to change their mind. A trusted friend, teacher, mentor, or other recipient
becomes a secondary stakeholder only when the writer chooses to export and
share an entry; private writing does not imply that another person is entitled
to access it.

Technical and organizational actors also influence the experience even when
they never read an entry directly. ReflectBlocks developers determine the
available controls and defaults. A storage provider may hold saved blocks or
entries, and an AI provider may process text when generation is requested.
These actors control infrastructure and policy, while the writer bears much of
the consequence of unauthorized disclosure, unclear retention, or distorted
output. Privacy and security specialists can examine the data lifecycle, and
people with expertise in journaling, writing, or wellbeing can identify harmful
assumptions in prompts. Such experts are advisers rather than substitutes for
the people who will actually use the system.

The initial concept rests on five assumptions. First, some prospective users
can capture a short thought more readily than they can begin a complete journal
entry. Second, prompts can support reflection when they are relevant and
optional. Third, users want assistance with organization without surrendering
authorship of the final text. Fourth, showing the source of generated passages
can help users inspect and correct the output. Fifth, clear storage, processing,
export, and deletion controls are necessary when entries contain personal
information. None of these statements is yet a finding. Each is recorded as a
provisional claim to be examined during data gathering rather than embedded
silently as a design fact.

These interests do not always align. A provider may prefer to retain data for
reliability, analytics, or model improvement, while a writer may expect an
unsaved or deleted thought to disappear. A system may favor smooth automation,
while the writer may need an interruption that makes AI processing and its
consequences visible. A generated entry may sound polished but introduce an
interpretation the writer did not intend. ReflectBlocks therefore treats
control as more than the presence of an edit button. The writer must be able to
understand what will happen, initiate consequential actions, inspect the result,
revise or reject it, and remove stored material. These principles follow the
broader HCI goal of making the conceptual model and available actions
understandable to the person using the system \cite{norman2013design}.

The problem space is deliberately narrower than digital mental-health support.
ReflectBlocks does not diagnose emotions, recommend treatment, evaluate whether
a reaction is correct, or intervene in a crisis. It should not infer that every
entry represents a stable psychological state. Prompts may help a writer examine
an experience, but they cannot guarantee insight or benefit. This boundary is
important because positioning a reflective writing tool as therapy would create
different safety, expertise, and evaluation requirements than those supported
by the present project.

\subsection{Current Practice and Opportunity Areas}

Because fieldwork has not yet been reported, the following journey is a
provisional current-practice model, not an observed universal sequence. A
prospective user experiences an event and decides that it is worth remembering.
They may write a sentence in a notes application, open a paper or digital
journal, use a fixed template, or postpone writing. If they continue, they must
decide which details matter, find words for reactions that may still be unclear,
and organize those fragments into an entry. Finally, they choose whether to
keep the writing private, share it, or discard it.

This journey exposes several possible breakdowns for investigation. The
motivation to capture the event may be brief, while composing a full entry
takes sustained attention. A blank page provides freedom but little support for
deciding what to explore. A fixed template provides structure but may ask
irrelevant questions or impose an order that does not match the writer's
experience. Postponing the task may reduce immediate effort but can also leave
only a minimal note whose meaning is difficult for the writer to reconstruct
later. These are reasoned possibilities derived from the proposed concept, not
participant findings; interviews and observation must establish when, for whom,
and with what consequences they actually occur. Contextual inquiry is relevant
because it can examine decisions near the situated activity rather than asking
people only to speculate about a finished interface \cite{beyer1998contextual}.

Three preliminary opportunity areas follow from this model. The first is
\emph{low-friction capture with progressive support}. A one-sentence starting
point could preserve the immediacy of a thought, while optional blocks could
help the writer expand it at their own pace. The key need is not necessarily a
particular set of cards; it is the ability to move between minimal capture,
guided reflection, and unstructured writing without losing work or being forced
through every step.

The second opportunity is \emph{assistance that preserves authorship}. Explicit
generation, editable output, and links from generated prose back to source
blocks could make organization inspectable rather than magical. The design
must still determine whether writers understand these links, whether generated
language changes the meaning or tone of their account, and whether a polished
draft discourages substantive editing. A successful interaction would let the
writer treat generation as a reversible drafting operation, not as an
authoritative interpretation of their experience.

The third opportunity is \emph{privacy as an interaction rather than a hidden
policy}. Before submitting personal text for processing, a user should be able
to learn what data will leave the device, why it is needed, where it will be
stored, and how it can be deleted. Retention choices should distinguish original
blocks from the generated entry and allow the user to save neither. These
controls create design questions about comprehensible wording, safe defaults,
confirmation of permanent deletion, and the behavior of backups or external AI
services.

Together, the three areas define the problem space carried into data gathering:
supporting the transition from a fleeting thought to deeper reflection,
maintaining the writer's authorship throughout system assistance, and making
the lifecycle of sensitive text understandable and controllable. The next stage
must test these opportunities against actual practices, including people who
prefer blank writing, people who reject generated prose, and people for whom
privacy concerns outweigh the value of automated organization.

\section{Data Gathering}

\subsection{Method Rationale and Study Design}

We chose interviews because they let people describe, in their own words, what
actually happens when they try to journal. We added a short card exercise at
the end of each interview: participants looked at sample ReflectBlocks
prompts on paper cards and said which ones felt useful and which did not.
This gave us a reaction to something concrete, not just a memory
\cite{preece2023interaction}.

We recruited participants mostly from our own families in Bangladesh,
especially parents and elderly relatives, all of them above age 45.
Recruitment and fieldwork took place over a two-month period. We looked for
people who journal in some form (paper, an app, voice notes) at least
sometimes, or who had tried and stopped. We wanted a mix of habits, not a large
or representative sample. Eight people took part, mostly above age 45: five
journal on and off, two used to journal but stopped in the last year, and one
writes daily using a fixed template. A ninth person had to cancel and was not
replaced. Because everyone who joined was already willing to talk about
journaling, our findings say less about people who have never tried it at all.

Before the interviews, we wrote down three things we were assuming: that a
short thought is easier to start than a full entry, that prompts only help
when they feel relevant, and that people want help organizing their writing
without losing control over it. After the interviews we checked each
assumption against what participants actually said.

\subsection{Procedure, Ethics, and Data Management}

Each session took about 20 minutes and was done in person, usually by visiting
participants at their homes. All interviews were conducted in Bangla;
quotations used in this report were translated or lightly paraphrased into
English while preserving the intended meaning. After consent, participants
answered a few quick questions about their journaling habits, did the main
interview (guide in Appendix
\ref{app:materials}), and then the card exercise. We took written session notes and kept the marked-up cards. Taking
part was voluntary: participants could skip a question or stop at any time,
and no one received payment or course credit for taking part. One person
skipped the card exercise because they were short on time but still did the
full interview.

We opened with simple, open questions like ``Tell me about the last time you
wrote about something that happened to you,'' then followed up with ``What
happened next?'' Asking about a real, specific moment worked better than
asking about habits in general. We tested the interview once with a lab
colleague first: the original opening question (``Tell me about your
reflection habits'') got vague answers, so we changed it to ask about one
specific time instead.

\section{Data Analysis, Interpretation, and Presentation}

\subsection{Preparation and Analytic Approach}

We used thematic analysis on the eight interview transcripts and the marked-up
cards. First we read through each transcript and wrote a short summary of it.
Then we labeled interesting parts with short codes, compared codes across
participants, and grouped similar codes into themes. We checked each theme
against the original transcripts to make sure it still matched what people
actually said \cite{braun2006thematic}.

Two of us coded the first three transcripts separately and then compared
notes. Some codes did not match at first, mostly around whether something
counted as a ``workaround'' or just plain ``friction,'' so we talked it
through and agreed on one shared set of codes. One person then coded the rest
and checked back on anything unclear. When a participant said something that
did not fit a theme, we kept it as an exception instead of ignoring it, and
those exceptions are noted in the findings below.

\subsection{Findings}
\label{sec:findings}

\paragraph{Theme 1: People lose the thought before they even start writing.}
Most participants said the hard part was not writing, it was getting to a
journal in time. P03 said, ``I have the thought in the shower or on the bus,
and by the time I sit at my laptop it's gone, or it feels like homework.''
Six of eight participants described the same thing: they had a thought, but
by the time they sat down to write, it had faded. This tells us the design
should focus on capturing a thought fast, before worrying about structure.

\paragraph{Theme 2: Prompts help, but only sometimes.} Most participants liked
having optional prompts, but only when the prompts matched how they felt. P02
said, ``The `what did you learn' question is nice sometimes, but other times
I just want to vent, and it makes me feel like I have to produce a lesson.''
One participant, P05, was the opposite: they used a fixed template every day
and liked always seeing the same questions. This tells us prompts should be
easy to skip, and that one default will not work for everyone.

\paragraph{Theme 3: People want to know what the system changed.} Participants
were fine with the system organizing their writing, as long as they could
tell what was still their own words. P06 said, ``If it changes my words even
a little, I want to know exactly which words are mine.'' Seven of eight
participants wanted a way to compare the generated text with what they
originally wrote, and one participant (P08) said they would only ever use
blank writing, no generated text at all. This tells us showing the source of
generated text is not a nice-to-have, and blank writing needs to stay a real
option, not just a backup.

\section{Establishing Requirements}

\subsection{From Findings to Requirements}

Requirements state what a system and its surrounding service must enable or
respect; they should not prematurely prescribe a screen layout
\cite{preece2023interaction}. We translated each supported finding into a
requirement by naming the actor, condition, action or quality, and success
criterion. Requirements were checked with [participants, stakeholders, expert,
or literature] through [walkthrough, prioritization activity, or review].

Functional requirements describe supported actions. Data requirements identify
the information needed, its source, ownership, sensitivity, and lifecycle.
Non-functional requirements express qualities such as accessibility, privacy,
reliability, learnability, and performance. Organizational requirements
describe policy, staffing, integration, or governance conditions. The four
categories can interact: an apparently simple reminder feature may create data,
consent, and notification constraints.

\subsection{Prioritization and Acceptance Criteria}

We prioritized requirements using [MoSCoW / value-risk matrix / stakeholder
workshop]. A ``Must'' item is essential for the defined release or study
scenario, not merely popular. Priority reflects evidence, consequence of
absence, implementation dependencies, and equity impacts. For each
high-priority requirement, specify a testable acceptance criterion: ``Given
[condition], [user] can [action] with [measure] without [harm].''

Requirements remain provisional when evidence is thin. R[ID] is flagged for
validation because [uncertainty]. This honest uncertainty is a useful design
result: it directs the next study rather than disguising a decision as fact.

\guidance{Use active, testable wording: ``The system shall enable [role] to
\ldots'' or ``[Role] needs to \ldots when \ldots''. Include the rationale and
evidence ID beside every important requirement. Do not invent requirements
merely to fill categories.}

\subsection{Evaluation Questions and Methods}

\section{Study Materials}
\label{app:materials}

\subsection{Participant Information and Consent Checklist}

Before beginning, provide: the study purpose; what participation involves;
duration; note-taking and data handling; foreseeable discomforts; benefits, if
any; voluntary participation and withdrawal process; contact details; and the
applicable approval statement. Do not include a participant's name, signature,
contact information, or other identifying data in this submitted report.

\subsection{Semi-Structured Interview Guide}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Tell me about the last time you [performed the focal activity]. What
  happened from beginning to end?
  \item What parts were easy, difficult, slow, risky, or frustrating? Can you
  show me an example?
  \item Which people, tools, documents, and information did you use? What
  happened when one was unavailable?
  \item What workarounds have you developed, and what do they cost you or
  others?
  \item If a new system supported this activity, what would it need to respect
  about your practice, privacy, access, or control?
  \item Is there anything important we did not ask? Which of your answers should
  we treat cautiously?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Interviewer reminders.} Ask neutral follow-ups (``Can you say
more?''); do not promise a feature; do not pressure participants to disclose
sensitive information; record analytic memos separately from factual notes.
